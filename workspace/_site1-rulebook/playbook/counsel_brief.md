# Brief for counsel: lead-generating websites for NYC-area home-service contractors

Prepared 2026-10-24 by the operator, not by a lawyer. Nothing here is legal advice and nothing has been reviewed by counsel. Facts below come from primary sources where marked (agency pages, statutes) and from secondary summaries otherwise; each says which. The operator's working assumption is always the more conservative reading, so the business can proceed safely while waiting for answers.

Companion documents: `Lead_Site_Playbook_v1.md` (the operating playbook; section numbers below refer to it) and `_research/R4`, `R5`, `R6`, `R7` (research briefs with sources).

## 1. The business in one page

- A small builder (the Operator) with a business partner (the Partner, who handles sales, ads budget and legal contacts) builds websites for **home-service contractors** (roofing and HVAC first; plumbing and general contractors later) in **Queens, Brooklyn, the Bronx and Long Island**, and runs the capture, listing, review and reporting system around them.
- The site is built with an AI coding assistant from a single record of verified facts about the client. Lead forms store a lead, alert the client and forward a copy to an automation tool. Calls go through a call-tracking number. After-hours, an answering service or an AI voice assistant may take messages.
- Price discussed: a flat site fee (about $500) with a builder share of 30 percent, plus a proposed monthly care fee. A second product, **microsites** (one service in one area, leased or sold per lead to a licensed contractor), is in scope but not yet built.
- The Operator never claims to be a contractor, never promises rankings, lead counts or AI citations, and never writes reviews or invents staff, addresses or licenses (playbook 26.1).

## 2. Questions, in priority order

Each question gives: why it matters, what was found, the working assumption, and what is needed back.

### A. Is the model lawful? (highest priority)

**A1. Does a website that only passes leads to licensed contractors need an NYC home-improvement license or any other license?**
Matters: playbook 25.5 and 26.2. Found: NYC Admin Code section 20-387(a), as reproduced by a secondary site, bars anyone from soliciting or negotiating a home-improvement contract without a license; the separate salesperson license was repealed in 2020 (DCWP rule, primary). No guidance either way was found about a non-contractor lead site (R5 6.5). Assumption: do not build any lead-sale microsite until answered. Needed: yes or no, and any conditions (disclosure, no negotiation of terms).

**A2. May the Operator be paid per job (a commission or revenue share) or per lead by a licensed contractor?**
Matters: 25.5, 27.3. Found: Admin Code 20-393(15)(b) says a salesperson may not accept or pay compensation from anyone other than the contractor represented (secondary text). Assumption: per-lead fees only, with a written definition of "qualified" (a call over 60 seconds), and no per-job fee until answered. Needed: whether per-lead and per-job models are each lawful and how to document them.

**A3. What disclosure must a lead site carry?** Matters: 25.5, 26.5. Assumption: a plain statement that the site connects visitors with a local licensed contractor, with a referral and advertising disclosure page in the footer, and never presenting itself as the contractor. Needed: required wording, and any FTC or New York endorsement and advertising disclosure.

**A4. What if a lead is routed to a contractor who turns out to be unlicensed or lapsed?** Matters: 16.9. Assumption: confirm the license in the public registry before the first lead and monthly. Needed: exposure and the clause that allocates it.

### B. What contractors may say in the sites we build

**B1. License number in advertising.** DCWP checklist (primary, updated 06/2026) says advertising must include the home improvement contractor license number (6 RCNY 2-222(a)). Assumption: show the real number in the header or footer and in every ad; never show one the client does not hold. Needed: whether a website counts as advertising for this rule, where the number must appear, and the format. In a sample of ten NYC contractor sites none showed a number (R6 Section 6).

**B2. Financing.** DCWP's checklist says a licensee "cannot arrange for financing or help a consumer obtain financing" (primary); Admin Code 20-393(4) and (17) bar acting as an agent for or promoting a lender (secondary reproduction). One of the ten sampled competitors advertised 0 percent financing. Assumption: no financing widgets, lender logos, "apply for financing" buttons or prequalification links for an NYC licensee. Needed: where the line falls (may a site say "payment options available, ask us"? may it link a third party that the customer contacts independently?).

**B3. Referral rewards.** Admin Code 20-393(10) and (16) (secondary text) restrict promising or paying owners credit or compensation for procuring contracts. Assumption: no "refer a neighbor, get $100" promotion. Needed: confirmation and any lawful form of a referral program.

**B4. Benefit and savings claims.** 6 RCNY 2-222(r) requires provable benefit claims (primary). Assumption: no unsourced savings or performance claims. Needed: acceptable substantiation.

**B5. Nassau, Suffolk, Westchester, Putnam, Rockland.** The Attorney General's fact sheet says contractors must be licensed in these counties (primary, undated). Nassau reportedly requires the number in display advertising (a 2014 regulation not re-opened); no Suffolk advertising rule was found. Assumption: show the county license number wherever the contractor advertises in that county, and advertise only where licensed (Appendix H.12.3). Needed: current advertising rules for each county.

**B6. Insurance-claim work.** Matters: Appendix H.11. Assumption: describe only what the client does (inspect, document, estimate, repair); never promise to negotiate with insurers, get a claim approved, or waive a deductible. Needed: what a contractor may say about insurance claims and deductibles in New York, and public-adjuster limits (Insurance Law section 2108; 11 NYCRR Part 25, per a 2008 DFS opinion found).

**B7. "Licensed and insured" wording.** Assumption: only with the license number shown and an insurance certificate on file. Needed: any required form of words.

**B8. Name rules.** Admin Code 20-393(8) bars operating under any name other than the licensed name. Assumption: one name on the license, truck, listing and site (Appendix H.12.2). Needed: whether a filed assumed name (DBA) alone satisfies it.

**B9. Home-improvement contract law (GBL Article 36-A).** Assumption: site copy never promises "no deposit", "pay later" or price locks the contract does not support. Needed: the dollar threshold below which the article does not apply (a county page says $500; not in the statute text read) and anything else the site must avoid.

### C. Calls, texts and automated voice

**C1. Call-recording consent and notice.** New York and New Jersey are one-party; Connecticut's civil statute (52-570d) reportedly requires all-party consent (guides dated 2019 and 2020, secondary). Assumption: recording off by default; where on, play a notice at the start and treat every call as all-party. Needed: notice wording and whether a recording notice is enough, for callers in NY, NJ and CT.

**C2. SMS and the TCPA.** The FCC one-to-one consent rule was vacated (24 Jan 2025). The right to revoke "by any reasonable means" stands, and a September 2026 FCC order may narrow it (effective date unconfirmed). NY GBL 399-z is reported to cover texts with an 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. window (secondary). Assumption: lead-facing texts only after A2P 10DLC registration and a separate unchecked consent box; send 9 a.m. to 8 p.m.; honor STOP for all messages. Needed: consent wording, revocation handling after the September 2026 order, and the New York statute's actual text.

**C3. AI voice assistant.** FCC 24-17 holds AI-generated voices are "artificial" under the TCPA, so initiating calls with them needs prior express consent (primary). Our reading: an assistant that answers a caller who dialed the business is not initiating. NY GBL 396-b (effective 2026-06-09) requires disclosure of a "synthetic performer" in an advertisement. Assumption: disclose on every call; no AI callbacks to leads without form consent. Needed: confirm the inbound reading; whether any New York, New Jersey or Connecticut rule requires a disclosure on inbound AI calls (none was found).

**C4. Review requests by text from the contractor's own phone.** Assumption: personal one-to-one texts to past customers carry lower risk than automated blasts, but this is our inference. Needed: whether such texts need consent, and whether any wording is required.

**C5. Email.** CAN-SPAM (primary): opt-outs within 10 business days. Needed: only confirmation that the operator's alert and acknowledgement emails, and any nurture emails, are compliant.

### D. Data, privacy and accessibility

**D1. SHIELD Act and breach notice.** Reportedly requires reasonable safeguards and notice within 30 days of discovery of a breach of "private information" (law-firm summaries; statute not read). The forms collect name, phone, optional email and ZIP, never SSN, card or license numbers. Needed: whether this data is in scope, vendor-contract requirements (hosting, database, email, automation tools), and a breach-plan template.

**D2. Retention and recordings.** Assumption: caller numbers truncated to area code after 90 days with a salted hash kept for matching; recordings kept for the period in the contract. Needed: acceptable retention periods and what the privacy policy must say.

**D3. Cookies and tracking.** No New York cookie-consent statute was found (absence of evidence). Assumption: cookieless analytics where possible, no ad pixels before consent, all tracking disclosed. Needed: confirmation, and whether a California-style wiretap risk matters for New York-only advertisers.

**D4. Accessibility.** New York is a high-volume venue for website-accessibility suits (vendor and law-firm counts). Assumption: build to WCAG 2.2 AA and publish a statement with a contact route. Needed: a response protocol for a demand letter (Appendix H.12.6) and what the statement should and should not say.

### E. Contracts and corporate

**E1. Client agreement.** Topics are listed in playbook Appendix E.5. Needed drafted: scope tiers (Launch, Care, Growth); the honest promise in 2.1 and the things never promised; client obligations and the pause and stop rules; ownership of the listing, domain, DNS, analytics and content, with repository transfer on final payment; the rule that the client owns the Cloudflare account and phone numbers; the hold or refund rule when intake stalls; paid-ads disclosure; AI-use disclosure; limits on liability; termination. The Operator will not hold a client's listing or number as leverage.

**E2. Agreement between the Operator and the Partner**, including the revenue split, who funds post-launch work, and who owns the automation layer.

**E3. Microsite and lead-lease contract** with a receiving contractor (only after A1 and A2 are answered).

**E4. Entity, assumed name and operator insurance.** The Partner is forming the legal entity; brand name is open. Needed: assumed-name filing, and whether professional-liability or errors-and-omissions cover and data-liability cover are advisable for this work.

### F. Reviews

**F1. FTC reviews rule (16 CFR Part 465).** Bans fake reviews, review gating and suppression, and sentiment-conditioned incentives (primary; penalty figure reported as $53,088 per knowing violation, found via a snippet). Google's own policy is stricter. Assumption: ask every customer identically, no incentives, no scripted wording, replies drafted by the Operator and posted by a person, no legal threats over reviews. Needed: sign-off on that workflow, and when a takedown demand is appropriate.
